Twenty-Seven on Nine Points

On nine points the largest 4-uniform sunflower-free family has exactly 27 members — and there are exactly two such families up to relabelling, one intersecting and one not.

1 · Definitions

Let [n] = {0,…,n−1} and let C([n],m) be the m-subsets of [n]. Three distinct sets form a 3-sunflower when

A ∩ B  =  A ∩ C  =  B ∩ C

The common value is the core, and it may be empty. A family is sunflower-free when no three members form one, and intersecting when no two members are disjoint.

g(m,n) = max { |F| : F ⊆ C([n],m), sunflower-free }
ι(m,n) = max { |F| : F ⊆ C([n],m), sunflower-free and intersecting }

Trivially ι(m,n) ≤ g(m,n). A family is a set of sets, so distinctness is automatic; the Coq development represents families as lists and so carries Distinct explicitly.

3 · Upper bound

Lemma 2 · link · Coq, closed

Let F ⊆ C([n],4) be sunflower-free and x ∈ [n]. Then L_x = { A∖{x} : A ∈ F, x ∈ A } is a sunflower-free subfamily of C([n]∖{x},3) with |L_x| = deg(x).

Proof

Each A∖{x} has three elements. If A∖{x} = B∖{x} with x ∈ A ∩ B then A = B, so the map is injective. If A∖{x}, B∖{x}, C∖{x} are distinct with common pairwise intersection Y, then A,B,C are distinct with common pairwise intersection Y ∪ {x}, contradicting sunflower-freeness of F. ∎

This is PureLink.link_at_point_bounded, which predates this note.

Theorem 3 · Coq, closed conditional on Prop 1

g(4,9) = ι(4,9) = 27.

Proof

Let F ⊆ C([9],4) be sunflower-free. By Lemma 2, deg(x) ≤ g(3,8) = 12. Counting the incidences {(x,A) : x ∈ A ∈ F} in both directions,

4·|F|  =  Σ deg(x)  ≤  9 · 12  =  108

so |F| ≤ 27. The family F₁ of §6 attains it, so g(4,9) = 27; F₁ is intersecting, so ι(4,9) ≥ 27, and ι ≤ g closes it. ∎

Corollary 4 · forced regularity · Coq, closed

Every 27-member sunflower-free F ⊆ C([9],4) is exactly 12-regular.

Proof

Σ deg(x) = 108 = 9·12 with every deg(x) ≤ 12 leaves no slack. ∎

Corollary 4 is what makes §5 terminate: a point reaching degree 12 is closed to every later member. Without the degree cap the same Case 1 search was attempted three times and terminated none of them — 110 s unpruned, 300 s with only the pair-degree bound, and a third run stopped by hand at about 330 s. With the cap it finishes in ≈75 s.

5 · Classification

Theorem 5 · computation, cross-checked

Up to relabelling there are exactly two sunflower-free families in C([9],4) of size 27: one intersecting (F₁) and one not (F₂). So the extremal family for ι(4,9) is unique, and for g(4,9) it is not.

The two cases are exhaustive and each was searched to the end.

Case 1 — F intersecting

Relabel a member onto A₀ = {0,1,2,3}. For any other member B, intersecting plus distinct gives j = |B ∩ A₀| ∈ {1,2,3}. The stabiliser Sym(A₀) × Sym([9]∖A₀) acts transitively on {B : |B ∩ A₀| = j}, so a relabelling fixing A₀ carries B onto

R₁ = {0,4,5,6}     R₂ = {0,1,4,5}     R₃ = {0,1,2,4}

Every intersecting extremal family therefore has a relabelling containing A₀ and some R_j. Searching all three with the degree cap of Corollary 4:

40 families found · 139 433 999 nodes · ≈75 s
orbits under Sym([9]): 1

Case 2 — F not intersecting

Then F has disjoint members A,B with |A ∪ B| = 8 ≤ 9, so a relabelling carries them onto {0,1,2,3} and {4,5,6,7}. Searching every family containing that pair:

144 families found · 15 640 126 124 nodes
orbits under Sym([9]): 1

The node count is from a completed run and is deterministic. Its wall time was not captured, and a timed re-run was still going at 39 minutes single-core when this was written — treat the cost as “tens of minutes” rather than any figure this note could stand behind.

Disjointness is genuinely possible here: three pairwise disjoint 4-sets need twelve points, so on nine an empty-core sunflower cannot occur and sunflower-freeness does not imply intersecting. That is exactly why Case 2 is non-empty.

5.1 Independent verification of both counts

Both searches were checked against a computation that does not use them. Orbits were generated by brute force over all 9! = 362 880 relabellings:

|orbit(F₁)| = 280     |Aut(F₁)| = 362880/280  = 1296
|orbit(F₂)| = 7560    |Aut(F₂)| = 362880/7560 =   48

From the orbit of F₁ alone, the labelled copies containing A₀ together with R_j number

j = 1 : 18      j = 2 : 10      j = 3 : 12      sum = 40

— exactly the Case 1 count. From the orbit of F₂ alone, the labelled copies containing a fixed disjoint pair number

7560 · 6 / 315  =  144

— exactly the Case 2 count. These predictions use only the orbits. Because the totals match rather than merely bound, they confirm the searches in both directions: nothing in those orbits was missed, and no further orbit meets either search space — a third class would have pushed the counts above 40 and 144.

10 · What is not proved

  1. Proposition 1 is a computation, not a proof object. The Coq theorems are explicitly conditional on GThreeOnEight. Two independent implementations agree and every SAT model was re-verified, but neither is a proof.
  2. The censuses of §5 are Rust programs. Their correctness rests on the symmetry reductions (proved above), the degree cap (Corollary 4, machine-checked), and the implementations. The orbit checks of §5.1 match exactly rather than merely bounding, but they are not a formal proof of the enumeration.
  3. The prior-art search is not exhaustive. See §12.
  4. Nothing here bears on ι(4) itself, on Erdős–Rado, or on the open ι(4,11) question; §7 gives the precise reason.

11 · Errata and methodological notes

A wrong cross-check nearly produced a false refutation

The first SAT check of Proposition 1 reported ≥13 satisfiable, contradicting the branch-and-bound result. The model was decoded before being believed and held only 12 sets: the hand-rolled counter was too weak, not the DFS wrong. It was rewritten as a standard Sinz counter and validated exhaustively on every n ≤ 7 and k ≤ n+2 against brute-force truth before reuse. A cross-check that disagrees with the thing it checks says nothing about that thing until the check itself has been checked.

A scratch script miscounted disjoint pairs. Iterating for A in F for B in F if A < B over Python frozensets uses < as proper subset, not as an order, and reported zero disjoint pairs for both families. Recounted with itertools.combinations: F₁ has 0, F₂ has 6 — which is what the Rust test asserted throughout. No published number was affected.

12 · Prior art

The outcome is a negative, so the scope of the search is given rather than the conclusion alone.

Searched: three targeted web searches on sunflower-free uniqueness and exact small values; the most recent survey in the area, Delta-system method: a survey (arXiv:2508.20132, August 2025), read specifically for exact values and uniqueness statements — it contains neither, only asymptotics and structural theorems. Earlier commissioned work covered arXiv full-text, Google Scholar forward-citation chains from Abbott–Hanson–Sauer 1972 and from Kostochka's survey, Springer, ScienceDirect, zbMATH, Semantic Scholar, and all 434 comments of the seven Polymath10 threads read first-hand.

The structural argument is stronger than any single search. A uniqueness theorem at (4,9) presupposes the value g(4,9) = 27. No exact value of this kind for k = 4 on a bounded ground set was located anywhere; the only exact value in the area is Abbott–Gardner's g(3) = 20 (1969), an unbounded-ground-set quantity. If the value is unpublished, a classification of its extremal families is too.

That is high confidence, not certainty. No search proves a negative, and this paragraph describes what was looked at, not what exists.

13 · Scope

This is a small, self-contained finite result. It settles nine points completely and, by §7, says nothing about ten or eleven. Its interest is that the extremal number is the same for the general and the intersecting problem while the extremal families are not: relaxing "intersecting" admits exactly one further family and no larger one.
